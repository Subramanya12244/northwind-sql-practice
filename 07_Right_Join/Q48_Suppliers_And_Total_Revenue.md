# Q48 · Suppliers and Total Revenue

> **Revision Card**
> **Category:** RIGHT JOIN | **Difficulty:** Medium
> **Concepts:** RIGHT JOIN (chained, two levels) · GROUP BY · SUM · NULL Handling (COALESCE) · ROUND/CAST
> **Outcome:** ✅ correct first attempt
> **Watch out for:** every join in the chain `order_details → products → suppliers` must be RIGHT (not INNER) — an INNER step drops never-ordered products and their suppliers vanish before the final join.

## 1. Problem

The procurement team wants a report showing every supplier and the total revenue generated from the products they supply. Suppliers with no product sales should also appear with a revenue of 0.

**Objective:** Return all suppliers along with the total revenue generated by their products, ensuring suppliers with no sales appear with 0.00.

**Tables:** `order_details`, `products`, `suppliers`

**Expected output:**

| Column Name | Description |
|-------------|-------------|
| company_name | Name of the supplier |
| total_revenue | Total discounted revenue from products supplied by this supplier (0.00 if no sales) |

**Sample output:**

| company_name | total_revenue |
|--------------|---------------|
| Plutzer Lebensmittelgroßmärkte AG | 74906.01 |
| Exotic Liquids | 24567.80 |
| Supplier X | 0.00 |

*(Sample values are illustrative, based on the standard Northwind dataset.)*

## 2. My Approach

1. Chain `order_details → products → suppliers` (right-to-left preservation).
2. First `RIGHT JOIN`: `order_details RIGHT JOIN products` on `product_id` — preserves all products, including those never ordered.
3. Second `RIGHT JOIN`: `[od + products] RIGHT JOIN suppliers` on `supplier_id` — preserves all suppliers, even those whose products have never been sold. Net effect: `suppliers` is fully preserved (the rightmost table).
4. Aggregate revenue with `SUM()`; `COALESCE` converts NULL to 0.00 for suppliers with no sales.
5. Cast to `numeric` and `ROUND` to 2 decimal places.
6. `GROUP BY s.company_name, s.supplier_id`.

## 3. My Solution (as submitted)

```sql
SELECT
    s.company_name,
    COALESCE(
        ROUND(
            SUM(od.quantity * od.unit_price * (1 - od.discount))::numeric,
            2
        ),
        0
    ) AS total_revenue
FROM order_details od
RIGHT JOIN products p
    ON p.product_id = od.product_id
RIGHT JOIN suppliers s
    ON s.supplier_id = p.supplier_id
GROUP BY
    s.company_name,
    s.supplier_id;
```

## 6. Why This Works

**Why the chain goes `order_details → products → suppliers`:** revenue lives in `order_details`, but there is no direct link between `order_details` and `suppliers`. The path requires two hops: `order_details.product_id → products.product_id → products.supplier_id → suppliers.supplier_id`. Two joins are needed, and both must be `RIGHT JOIN` to preserve `suppliers` (the final right table) all the way through.

**Understanding the two-level RIGHT JOIN chain:**
- First `RIGHT JOIN`: `order_details` (left) RIGHT JOIN `products` (right) — preserves all products, including those never ordered.
- Second `RIGHT JOIN`: `[order_details + products]` (left) RIGHT JOIN `suppliers` (right) — preserves all suppliers, including those whose products have never been ordered.

The net result: every supplier appears, regardless of whether their products have ever been sold.

**Why `COALESCE` is required:** `SUM()` returns NULL for suppliers whose products have no matching `order_details` rows. `COALESCE` converts that NULL to `0.00` as required.

**LEFT JOIN equivalent (same result, arguably clearer):**
```sql
SELECT
    s.company_name,
    COALESCE(ROUND(SUM(od.quantity * od.unit_price * (1 - od.discount))::numeric, 2), 0) AS total_revenue
FROM suppliers s
LEFT JOIN products p ON p.supplier_id = s.supplier_id
LEFT JOIN order_details od ON od.product_id = p.product_id
GROUP BY s.company_name, s.supplier_id;
```

**Why `GROUP BY s.company_name, s.supplier_id`:** `supplier_id` is the unique key; `company_name` is included as a non-aggregated selected column. Grouping on `company_name` alone risks merging two suppliers with identical names.

## 7. Common Pitfalls (generic)

- Reversing the chain direction — placing `suppliers` on the left and `order_details` on the right with `RIGHT JOIN` at both steps would preserve `order_details`, not `suppliers`.
- Converting either `RIGHT JOIN` to `INNER JOIN`, which drops suppliers with no product sales at that step.
- Forgetting `COALESCE` — `SUM()` returns NULL (not 0) for empty groups.
- Grouping by `company_name` alone, risking silent row merges for suppliers with identical names.

## 8. Interview Follow-ups

**1. Why does the chain go `order_details → products → suppliers` and not `suppliers → products → order_details`?**

With `RIGHT JOIN`, the table you want to fully preserve must be the rightmost table in the final join. Since the requirement is to preserve all suppliers, `suppliers` must be on the right. Working backwards: `products` is the bridge between `order_details` and `suppliers`, so it sits in the middle. The chain reads right-to-left in terms of "what's being preserved at each step": first `products` (relative to `order_details`), then `suppliers` (relative to the combined result). If you were using `LEFT JOIN` instead, the chain would read left-to-right in the more natural order: `suppliers → products → order_details`.

**2. What does the first RIGHT JOIN (order_details → products) preserve, and why does it matter?**

The first `RIGHT JOIN` preserves all products, including those with no `order_details` rows (never-ordered products). This matters because a supplier could have products in the catalog that have never been ordered — those products must survive into the second join so their supplier can still appear in the final result. If the first join were `INNER JOIN`, never-ordered products would be dropped, and their suppliers would then have no rows to carry forward — potentially disappearing from the result even though the second `RIGHT JOIN` is trying to preserve them.

**3. Is there a scenario where `company_name` alone would be an unsafe GROUP BY key?**

Yes — if two different suppliers happen to have the same `company_name`. This is more common than it seems in real-world supplier data (e.g. regional branches of the same company registered separately). Grouping on `company_name` alone would silently merge their rows and combine their revenue figures into one row, producing incorrect totals. Always include the primary key (`supplier_id`) in `GROUP BY` alongside display columns.

**4. How would you extend this to also show the number of distinct products each supplier contributes?**

Add `COUNT(DISTINCT p.product_id)` to the `SELECT`. Since `products` is already in the join chain (as the intermediate table), `p.product_id` is available without any additional joins:

```sql
SELECT
    s.company_name,
    COUNT(DISTINCT p.product_id) AS product_count,
    COALESCE(ROUND(SUM(od.quantity * od.unit_price * (1 - od.discount))::numeric, 2), 0) AS total_revenue
FROM order_details od
RIGHT JOIN products p ON p.product_id = od.product_id
RIGHT JOIN suppliers s ON s.supplier_id = p.supplier_id
GROUP BY s.company_name, s.supplier_id
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC;
```

**5. How would you find the supplier with the highest average revenue per product?**

Use a CTE to compute total revenue and product count per supplier, then divide:

```sql
WITH supplier_stats AS (
    SELECT
        s.company_name,
        s.supplier_id,
        COALESCE(SUM(od.quantity * od.unit_price * (1 - od.discount)), 0) AS total_revenue,
        COUNT(DISTINCT p.product_id) AS product_count
    FROM order_details od
    RIGHT JOIN products p ON p.product_id = od.product_id
    RIGHT JOIN suppliers s ON s.supplier_id = p.supplier_id
    GROUP BY s.company_name, s.supplier_id
)
SELECT
    company_name,
    ROUND((total_revenue / NULLIF(product_count, 0))::numeric, 2) AS avg_revenue_per_product
FROM supplier_stats
ORDER BY avg_revenue_per_product DESC NULLS LAST;
```

`NULLIF(product_count, 0)` guards against division by zero for suppliers with no products.

## 9. Key Takeaway

In a three-table RIGHT JOIN chain, the preserved entity is the rightmost, and every intermediate join must also be RIGHT to avoid dropping rows before the final join. The chain `A → B → C` is the exact mirror of the LEFT JOIN chain `C → B → A`.
