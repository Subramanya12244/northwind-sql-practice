# Q16 · Customers Above Average Revenue (Correlated Subquery)

> **Revision Card**
> **Category:** Correlated Subqueries | **Difficulty:** Medium
> **Concepts:** INNER JOIN (multi-table) · GROUP BY · HAVING · Subquery (non-correlated) · Aggregates (SUM, AVG)
> **Outcome:** ✅ correct first attempt
> **Watch out for:** the revenue formula is duplicated in the outer `HAVING` and the inner subquery — keep the two copies in sync.

## 1. Problem

Same business need as Q6 — flag above-average-revenue customers — solved via `HAVING` and a nested subquery rather than a CTE, for comparison.

**Objective:** Return customers whose total revenue exceeds the average revenue across all customers, using a `HAVING` clause referencing a subquery.

**Tables:** `customers`, `orders`, `order_details`

**Expected output:**

| Column Name | Description |
|-------------|-------------|
| customer_id | Unique identifier of the customer |
| company_name | Name of the customer's company |
| total_revenue | Sum of discounted revenue generated by this customer |

| customer_id | company_name | total_revenue |
|-------------|--------------|---------------|
| QUICK | QUICK-Stop | 117483.39 |
| ERNSH | Ernst Handel | 104874.98 |

## 2. My Approach

1. Join `customers`, `orders`, and `order_details`; aggregate discounted revenue per customer.
2. In `HAVING`, compare that total against a subquery that independently recomputes revenue per customer and averages it.
3. Sort by total revenue descending.

## 3. My Solution (as submitted)

```sql
SELECT
    cu.customer_id,
    cu.company_name,
    SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) AS total_revenue
FROM customers cu
JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = cu.customer_id
JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
GROUP BY cu.customer_id, cu.company_name
HAVING SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) > (
    SELECT AVG(rev)
    FROM (
        SELECT SUM(od2.unit_price * od2.quantity * (1 - od2.discount)) AS rev
        FROM orders o2
        JOIN order_details od2 ON od2.order_id = o2.order_id
        GROUP BY o2.customer_id
    ) sub
)
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC;
```

## 6. Why This Works

**Why the revenue expression must be repeated identically in both the outer `HAVING` and the inner derived table:** SQL doesn't let you reference an outer `SELECT`'s computed alias inside `HAVING`'s own comparison value in this structure — the inner subquery has to independently recompute customer revenue from scratch (joining `orders` and `order_details` again) to produce the company-wide average, since it's logically a separate, self-contained calculation over a different grouping scope (all customers) than the outer query's current group (one customer).

**Trade-off versus the CTE version (Q6):** this version recomputes the same revenue logic twice in the query text, which is more error-prone to maintain (if the revenue formula changes, it must be updated in two places) — this is one of the strongest practical arguments for preferring CTEs in real production code.

## 7. Common Pitfalls (generic)

- Letting the revenue formula drift out of sync between the outer `HAVING` and the inner subquery — e.g. fixing a discount bug in one but not the other.
- Forgetting `HAVING` (not `WHERE`) is required since the filter target is an aggregate.

## 8. Interview Follow-ups

**1. What's the main maintenance risk of this subquery approach compared to the CTE version in Q6?**

The revenue formula `SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount))` appears twice — once in the outer `HAVING` and again inside the nested derived table. If the formula ever changes (for example to add freight or fix the discount handling), you must update both copies identically; updating one and forgetting the other produces a silent logic bug where the per-customer total and the average are computed on different bases. The CTE version in Q6 defines the revenue once and references it, eliminating that drift risk.

**2. Why must the revenue calculation be duplicated here, rather than referencing the outer query's computed total directly?**

The outer query's `total_revenue` alias is a per-customer aggregate for the single group currently being evaluated, whereas the subquery needs the average across *all* customers — a different grouping scope. You cannot reference the outer aggregate alias inside `HAVING`'s comparison value in this structure, and even if you could, it would be the wrong grain. The inner subquery therefore has to independently re-join `orders` and `order_details` and recompute every customer's revenue from scratch to derive the company-wide average.

**3. Which version would you choose in a production codebase, and why?**

The CTE version (Q6), almost always. It computes the revenue expression in one named place, so the logic cannot drift between copies, and it reads top-to-bottom as a clear pipeline. Production code is read and modified far more often than it is written, so the maintainability advantage outweighs the stylistic familiarity of a nested `HAVING` subquery.

**4. How would query performance compare between this version and the CTE version, in principle?**

In principle they are very close: both must scan `orders` and `order_details`, aggregate revenue per customer, and compute the average. PostgreSQL does not cache the CTE result between the two uses unless materialized, and this subquery version likewise computes the average once as an initplan since it is non-correlated. Any real difference comes down to the planner's chosen join order and whether the aggregation is shared, not to the CTE-vs-subquery syntax itself.

## 9. Key Takeaway

A `HAVING` + subquery can replace a CTE for 'above average' filters, but it forces you to duplicate the revenue logic — a real maintainability cost that usually favors the CTE in production.
